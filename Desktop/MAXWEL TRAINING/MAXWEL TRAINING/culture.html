THE CULTURE AND TRADITION OF THE KOM COMMUNITY

The Afo‑A‑Kom (or Mbang in the Kom language) is a sacred, life‑size wooden statue symbolizing royal authority, unity, and succession. Its face is covered in copper and the body is decorated with beads; the base rests on carved buffalo heads . Stolen in 1966 and returned to Laikom in 1973 after international efforts, the statue is now exhibited annually and is believed to possess mystical powers. Only the Fon sees it once during his coronation, making it deeply sacred.


The Fon (king) oversees a hierarchical yet consultative governance structure supported by the Kwifoyn (traditional council) and Nchidoh (elders with red-feathered hats) . Ancestral skulls of lineage heads are preserved in sacred shrines and are central to spiritual identity. Libation rituals compensate for missing ancestors. Traditional Ndop cloth (blue‑black woven fabric) is worn during ceremonies, often paired with red caps and beads to signify status, especially by Fons and nobles. Artwork—including masks, ivory carvings, beaded stools and statues—often depicts the Fon or lineage ancestors and encodes spiritual significance . Anlu is a powerful traditional practice where groups of Kom women mobilize to shame wrongdoers—especially in cases like abuse or incest. The practice originated from legends of women dressing as warriors to repel invaders—and resurfaced politically in 1958–61 to challenge colonial rule and local injustices, disrupting courts, schools, markets, and institutions .
