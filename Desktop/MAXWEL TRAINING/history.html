THE HISTORY OF KOM

he Kom trace their roots to the Upper Mbam region in Tikar.
In the 19th century, they migratedbr south in search of fertile land and initially settled in Babessi, where conflict arose with the local ruler.
In a tragic event, the Fon of Babessi tricked and burned many Kom men in two separate rooms, as Kom’s male population grew.
The Kom Fon (Njinabo I), deceived, agreed to the act. After realizing it was a betrayal and discovering the Fon had escaped through a secret door,
he committed suicide—leaving no heir.
According to legend, a python spirit then guided the survivors to Laikom, their current capital
.The Fon (king) held supreme authority—both secular and sacred—supported by a council of elders known as the Kwifoyn or Quifon, and the Nchidoh (recognizable by red-feathered hats), who advised him. Village heads served as spiritual and administrative leaders under
this hierarchy.
This structure functioned similarly to a state, and secret “regulatory societies” such as the KwifoynBR had checks on the Fon’s power—reflecting a balance of centralized authority and communal oversight.

Under Fon Ndi (from 1926), Christianity was legalized and churches were built, weakening the traditional Fon’s authority.
As colonial-era institutions like schools and churches took root, the Fon’s influence gradually diminished during the 1960s and 1970s.
